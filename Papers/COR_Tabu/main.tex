%% Abstract
\begin{abstract}
The Job Shop Scheduling Problem with interval-valued processing times (IJSP) models real-world uncertainty where task durations are unknown but bounded. Comparing schedules then requires an interval ranking operator, and the classical notion of critical path must be extended to both extreme scenarios. I formalise \emph{extreme critical paths} --- paths critical in the best-case or worst-case scenario graph --- and use them to define a neighbourhood $H(\sigma)$ for local search. I prove that $H(\sigma)$ is feasible, connected and loses no improving neighbours under any admissible interval ranking. Five neighbourhood variants ($N_1$, $N_2$, $N_3$, $N_{\rm ext}$, $N_8$) are formulated within this framework and combined with four ranking operators (EV, LEX1, LEX2, YX) on 82 benchmark instances of up to 1000 operations. Two findings are noteworthy: \emph{(i)} after irace-based tabu search tuning, $N_2$ and $N_8$ emerge as the two strongest neighbourhoods (statistically tied, negligible effect $r=0.07$), both outperforming $N_{\rm ext}$ by a medium effect and $N_1$ by a large effect; $N_3$ is weakest by a large margin. \emph{(ii)} LEX2 yields makespan intervals 5--11\% narrower than the alternatives on average; a float analysis of both extreme graphs shows LEX2 solutions concentrate critical operations in the worst-case graph, consistent with LEX2 directly tightening the worst-case critical path. \emph{(iii)} On the full 82-instance IJSP benchmark, the irace-tuned TS-$N_2$ reduces mean RE\,(\%) by 56.6\% on the 12 classical instances and 57.7\% on the 70 Taillard instances relative to the previous best published solver; the neighbourhood ranking is consistent across all instance size classes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec1}

The Job Shop Scheduling Problem (JSP) is a flagship combinatorial optimisation problem in operations research, and neighbourhood-based local search has been its most competitive algorithmic paradigm for three decades \cite{vanLaarhoven1992,Nowicki1996,Nowicki2005}. The effectiveness of these methods rests almost entirely on neighbourhood design: the critical-block insight of \cite{Nowicki1996} --- that reversing interior arcs of a critical block cannot improve the makespan --- reduces neighbourhood size by two-thirds without sacrificing solution quality, and the extended neighbourhood of \cite{Nowicki2005} refines this further with a heads-and-tails viability filter. In practice, however, processing times are rarely known precisely at planning time: operator variability, machine condition, and material heterogeneity introduce uncertainty that deterministic models cannot capture.

A natural and tractable representation for such uncertainty is the \emph{closed interval} $[p^-, p^+]$, bounding the minimum and maximum possible durations \cite{Moore2009,Lei2012c}. The resulting \emph{Interval Job Shop Scheduling Problem (IJSP)} raises two intertwined challenges: comparing schedules requires an \emph{interval ranking operator} \cite{Bustince2013,Karmakar2012}, since the makespan is now an interval; and the critical-path concept that underpins efficient neighbourhood design must be reconsidered, because different paths may become critical under different realisations of the processing times \cite{Dubois2003,Artigues2015,GonzalezRodriguez2008}.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec1.1}

This paper makes the following contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Formal definition of extreme criticality for IJSP (Section~\ref{sec3}). I define a task or arc as \emph{extreme critical} if it lies on a critical path of the best-case graph $G^-(\sigma)$ or the worst-case graph $G^+(\sigma)$, and prove that this definition encompasses all necessarily critical tasks and is contained within the set of possibly critical tasks, making it a computationally tractable and theoretically sound approximation of the general criticality concept of \cite{Artigues2015}.

\item Neighbourhood $H(\sigma)$ based on extreme critical arcs (Section~\ref{sec4}). I define $H(\sigma)$ as the set of all feasible processing orders reachable by reversing a single extreme critical disjunctive arc, and prove three structural properties: feasibility (every move yields a feasible order), no loss of improving neighbours (moves outside $H(\sigma)$ cannot improve the makespan under any admissible ranking), and connectivity (every non-optimal order can reach a global optimum via a finite sequence of moves in $H(\sigma)$).

\item Five neighbourhood variants within the $H(\sigma)$ framework (Section~\ref{sec5}). I formulate $N_1$, $N_2$, $N_3$, $N_{\rm ext}$ and a new variant $N_8$ (boundary arc swaps extended with extra-block reinsertion moves, after~\cite{Xie2022}) as instances of $H(\sigma)$, characterising their theoretical size and the cost of generating them.

\item Empirical study on 82 benchmark instances (Section~\ref{sec6}). Three experimental phases are reported: \emph{(a)} a $5 \times 4$ comparison of four ranking operators at a common parameter setting (Section~\ref{sec6.2}); \emph{(b)} a head-to-head comparison of the five neighbourhoods after irace-based hyperparameter tuning, isolating the structural effect of the neighbourhood (Section~\ref{sec6.5}); and \emph{(c)} a comparison against the state of the art (Section~\ref{sec6.7}), showing that the irace-tuned TS-$N_2$ achieves strictly better average solution quality than the previous best published solver.

\item Discovery and structural explanation of an interval-width effect (Section~\ref{sec6.2}). One ranking operator consistently yields makespan intervals narrower than the alternatives. A float analysis of both extreme graphs reveals the structural mechanism: the operator concentrates critical operations in the worst-case graph $G^+(\sigma)$, directly tightening the worst-case critical path.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec2}

\subsection{The Deterministic Job Shop Problem}
\label{sec2.1}

The JSP consists of $n$ jobs $\{J_1,\ldots,J_n\}$ and $m$ machines $\{M_1,\ldots,M_m\}$. Each job $J_i$ is a sequence of operations $O_{i,1},\ldots,O_{i,m}$, where operation $O_{i,j}$ must be processed on machine $M_j$ for exactly $p_{i,j}$ time units. Operations within a job must respect their precedence order, and each machine processes at most one operation at a time. The goal is to find a \emph{feasible processing order} $\sigma$ minimising the makespan $C_{\max}$.

The \emph{solution graph} $G(\sigma) = (V, A \cup D(\sigma))$ represents a feasible order: $V$ is the set of operations augmented with a dummy source node $S$ and sink node $T$; $A$ contains precedence arcs within jobs; and $D(\sigma)$ contains disjunctive arcs imposed by the order $\sigma$ on each machine. The makespan equals the length of the longest path in $G(\sigma)$. The classical insight of \cite{vanLaarhoven1992} is that the neighbourhood for local search should restrict to reversals of arcs on critical paths, ensuring feasibility, while \cite{Nowicki1996} shows that reversals of \emph{interior} arcs of a critical block cannot improve the makespan, allowing further pruning.

\subsection{Interval Arithmetic and Ranking Operators}
\label{sec2.2}

When processing times are uncertain, I represent each as $\mathbf{p}=[p^-, p^+] \in \mathbb{IR}$, where $\mathbb{IR}$ denotes the set of closed intervals. The IJSP requires three arithmetic operations on $\mathbb{IR}$ \cite{Moore2009}:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}        &= [a^- + b^-,\, a^+ + b^+], \\
\max(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})    &= [\max(a^-,b^-),\, \max(a^+,b^+)].
\end{align}

Since there is no natural total order on $\mathbb{IR}$, comparing schedules requires choosing a \emph{ranking operator}. For any interval $\mathbf{a}=[a^-,a^+]$, define its \emph{midpoint} $m(\mathbf{a})=\tfrac{a^-+a^+}{2}$ and \emph{width} $w(\mathbf{a})=a^+-a^-$. I consider four rankings widely used in the literature \cite{Bustince2013,Xu2006,Lei2012c}:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{a} \leq_{\rm LEX1} \mathbf{b} &\iff a^- < b^- \,\vee\, (a^- = b^- \wedge a^+ \leq b^+) \\
\mathbf{a} \leq_{\rm LEX2} \mathbf{b} &\iff a^+ < b^+ \,\vee\, (a^+ = b^+ \wedge a^- \leq b^-) \\
\mathbf{a} \leq_{\rm YX}   \mathbf{b} &\iff m(\mathbf{a}) < m(\mathbf{b}) \,\vee\, \bigl(m(\mathbf{a}) = m(\mathbf{b}) \wedge w(\mathbf{a}) \leq w(\mathbf{b})\bigr) \\
\mathbf{a} \leq_{\rm EV}   \mathbf{b} &\iff m(\mathbf{a}) \leq m(\mathbf{b})
\end{align}
LEX1, LEX2 and YX are \emph{admissible} total orders on $\mathbb{IR}$ \cite{Bustince2013}: they refine the partial product order $\leq_{2}$ defined by $\mathbf{a} \leq_{2} \mathbf{b} \iff a^- \leq b^- \wedge a^+ \leq b^+$. EV ranks intervals by their midpoint $m(\mathbf{a})$ and induces a total preorder rather than a strict total order, since two distinct intervals may share the same midpoint; it also refines $\leq_{2}$. This refinement property is essential for the theoretical guarantees of Section~\ref{sec4}: any move that simultaneously decreases both $C^-_{\max}$ and $C^+_{\max}$ is an improvement under all four rankings considered here.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:rw}

\paragraph{Neighbourhood structures for fuzzy and interval JSP.}
The first neighbourhood for the fuzzy/interval JSP was proposed in \cite{GonzalezRodriguez2008}, extending the van Laarhoven neighbourhood \cite{vanLaarhoven1992} to use possibly critical arcs rather than necessarily critical ones. This corresponds to what I call $N_1$ in the present framework. Subsequent work introduced $N_2$ as a filtered version of $N_1$ that retains only block-boundary arcs \cite{N2fjsp}, reducing neighbourhood size by $\sim$64\% with statistically identical solution quality on the fuzzy JSP. $N_3$ extends $N_2$ with three-task rearrangements at block boundaries \cite{N3fjsp}. For the related fuzzy job shop, evolutionary tabu search combining these neighbourhood structures with genetic operators has been proposed for due-date satisfaction objectives~\cite{Vela2020}. Lexicographic goal-programming strategies have also been explored for the green fuzzy FJSP with vague production targets~\cite{GarciaGomez2026Ann} and for the green interval FJSP combining makespan and energy objectives~\cite{Afsar2025}. Prior approaches to the IJSP specifically have combined neighbourhood search with genetic algorithms for makespan minimisation~\cite{DiazIPMU2020} and tardiness~\cite{DiazHAIS2020}, providing early evidence that the choice of ranking operator influences solution quality.

The Nowicki--Smutnicki extended neighbourhood~\cite{Nowicki2005}, adapted here as $N_{\rm ext}$, was originally proposed for the crisp JSP and achieves strong empirical performance by additionally considering interior block arcs that pass a heads-and-tails viability check. Its adaptation to the IJSP is a contribution of this work, exploiting the fact that the boundary-arc result of~\cite{Nowicki1996} can be lifted simultaneously to both extreme graphs $G^-(\sigma)$ and $G^+(\sigma)$. A fifth variant, $N_8$, is also proposed here: it extends $N_2$ with extra-block reinsertion moves that relocate critical block endpoints to positions outside the block, adapting the idea of~\cite{Xie2022} --- originally developed for the crisp JSP --- to the interval setting. For the deterministic JSP and FJSP, general frameworks for feasibility checking and quality evaluation of neighbourhood moves have been proposed in~\cite{Tamssaouet2023,GarciaGomez2026}; analogous efficiency improvements for the interval setting remain an open direction. In a complementary line, machine learning methods have been proposed to estimate quantile-based risk measures of the IJSP makespan~\cite{Calamita2025}.

\paragraph{Metaheuristic solvers for the IJSP.}
The fast-elitist Artificial Bee Colony ($ABC_{E3}$)~\cite{DiazFEABC2023} established best-known results on the 82-instance benchmark used here, combining ABC exploration with hill-climbing local search. The Elitist-Selection ABC ($ESABC$)~\cite{DiazICAE2023} further improved solution quality by incorporating a simulated-annealing diversity mechanism, at the cost of increased runtime. These two methods, together with the earlier GA of~\cite{DiazIPMU2020}, constitute the state of the art against which the proposed tabu search is compared in Section~\ref{sec6.7}.


\section{Extreme Criticality for IJSP}
\label{sec3}

\subsection{Configurations and Extreme Scenario Graphs}
\label{sec3.1}

A \emph{configuration} $\omega = (p_1,\ldots,p_o)$, where $o = n \times m$, assigns a deterministic duration $p_i \in \mathbf{p}_i$ to each operation~$i$; let $\Omega$ denote the set of all configurations. Given a feasible processing order $\sigma$, two \emph{extreme scenario graphs} are of particular interest:
\begin{itemize}
\item $G^-(\sigma)$: identical to $G(\sigma)$ but with each arc $(x,y)$ weighted by $p_{x}^{-}$ (best case).
\item $G^+(\sigma)$: identical to $G(\sigma)$ but with each arc $(x,y)$ weighted by $p_{x}^{+}$ (worst case).
\end{itemize}
The makespan interval satisfies
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{C}_{\max}(\sigma) = [C^-_{\max}(\sigma),\, C^+_{\max}(\sigma)],
\end{equation}
where $C^-_{\max}(\sigma)$ is the length of the longest path in $G^-(\sigma)$ and $C^+_{\max}(\sigma)$ is the length of the longest path in $G^+(\sigma)$. An illustrative example is shown in Figure~\ref{fig1}: two jobs ($J_1$: $O_{11}(M_1,[2,6])\!\to\!O_{12}(M_2,[2,3])$; $J_2$: $O_{21}(M_2,[4,4])\!\to\!O_{22}(M_1,[1,4])$), with schedule $\sigma$ fixing $M_1$ order $(O_{11},O_{22})$ and $M_2$ order $(O_{21},O_{12})$. In $G^-(\sigma)$ the critical path runs through the $M_2$ disjunctive arc ($C^-_{\max}=6$); in $G^+(\sigma)$ it runs through the $M_1$ disjunctive arc ($C^+_{\max}=10$). The critical arcs in the two extreme graphs differ, motivating the need to consider both.

\subsection{Heads, Tails and Floats}
\label{sec3.2}

For each extreme graph $G^{*}(\sigma)$ ($* \in \{-,+\}$), the \emph{head} $r_{x}^{*}$ of task $x$ is the length of the longest path from the dummy start node to $x$, and the \emph{tail} $q_{x}^{*}$ is the length of the longest path from the completion of $x$ to the end node. The \emph{float} of $x$ in $G^{*}(\sigma)$ is
\begin{equation}
F_{x}^{*}(\sigma) = C^{*}_{\max}(\sigma) - r_{x}^{*} - p_{x}^{*} - q_{x}^{*}.
\end{equation}
A task is critical in $G^{*}(\sigma)$ iff $F_{x}^{*}(\sigma) = 0$. The general problem of computing the minimum float $\min_{\omega\in\Omega} F_x(\sigma,\omega)$ over all configurations --- which equals zero iff $x$ is possibly critical --- is NP-hard \cite{Fortin2010,Dubois2005}; efficient branch-and-bound algorithms for this harder problem are given in \cite{Garaix2013}. The extreme-scenario floats $F_x^-$ and $F_x^+$ used here are computable in linear time and provide the tractable approximation exploited in Sections~\ref{sec4} and~\ref{sec5}.

\subsection{Definition and Connection to Possibly/Necessarily Critical Tasks}
\label{sec3.3}

\begin{definition}[Extreme critical path]
\label{def1}
A path $P$ in $G(\sigma)$ is \emph{extreme critical} if it is critical in $G^-(\sigma)$ or in $G^+(\sigma)$. A node, arc or block is extreme critical if it lies on an extreme critical path.
\end{definition}

This definition connects to the general criticality concepts of \cite{Fortin2010,Artigues2015} as follows. A task~$x$ is \emph{possibly critical} under $\sigma$ if there exists $\omega \in \Omega$ such that $F_x(\sigma,\omega)=0$; it is \emph{necessarily critical} if $F_x(\sigma,\omega)=0$ for every $\omega \in \Omega$ \cite{Fortin2010,Artigues2015}.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop1}
Every extreme critical task is possibly critical. Every necessarily critical task is extreme critical.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\textit{(i) Extreme critical $\Rightarrow$ possibly critical.}
Let task $x$ be extreme critical; then $F_x^-(\sigma)=0$ or $F_x^+(\sigma)=0$.
The extreme configurations $\omega^- = (p_i^-)_{i}$ and $\omega^+ = (p_i^+)_{i}$ both belong to $\Omega$
(since $p_i^{\pm}\in[p_i^-,p_i^+]$ by definition).
In case $F_x^-(\sigma)=0$: the float of $x$ under configuration $\omega^-$ equals
$F_x(\sigma,\omega^-)=C^-_{\max}(\sigma)-r_x^- - p_x^- - q_x^- = F_x^-(\sigma)=0$,
so $x$ lies on a critical path for $\omega^-\in\Omega$, making it possibly critical \cite{Fortin2010,Artigues2015}.
The case $F_x^+(\sigma)=0$ is analogous with $\omega^+$.

\textit{(ii) Necessarily critical $\Rightarrow$ extreme critical.}
If $x$ is necessarily critical, then $F_x(\sigma,\omega)=0$ for every $\omega\in\Omega$.
Taking $\omega=\omega^-\in\Omega$ gives $F_x^-(\sigma)=0$, so $x$ is critical in $G^-(\sigma)$, hence extreme critical.
\end{proof}

\medskip

The practical consequence is that extreme criticality offers a computationally tractable approximation to the exact criticality predicates: computing critical paths in $G^-(\sigma)$ and $G^+(\sigma)$ each costs $O(|A \cup D(\sigma)|)$ via standard longest-path algorithms, while computing the full set of possibly critical tasks is NP-hard in general \cite{Fortin2010,Artigues2015}.


\section{The Neighbourhood $H(\sigma)$ and Its Properties}
\label{sec4}

\subsection{Definition}
\label{sec4.1}

\begin{definition}[Neighbourhood $H(\sigma)$]
\label{def2}
Let $\sigma_{(v)}$ denote the processing order obtained from $\sigma$ by reversing a single disjunctive arc $v \in D(\sigma)$. The neighbourhood is
\begin{equation}
H(\sigma) = \{ \sigma_{(v)} \,:\, v \in D(\sigma) \text{ is extreme critical}\}.
\end{equation}
For comparison, the corresponding neighbourhood based on \emph{possibly} critical arcs is
\begin{equation}
H'(\sigma) = \{ \sigma_{(v)} \,:\, v \in D(\sigma) \text{ is possibly critical}\}.
\end{equation}
By Proposition~\ref{prop1}, $H(\sigma) \subseteq H'(\sigma)$, with strict inclusion in general.
\end{definition}

\subsection{No Loss of Improving Neighbours}
\label{sec4.2}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop2}
Let $\sigma \in \Sigma$ be a feasible order and let $v \in D(\sigma)$ be a disjunctive arc that is \emph{not} extreme critical in $G(\sigma)$. Then
\begin{equation}
C^-_{\max}(\sigma) \leq C^-_{\max}(\sigma_{(v)}) \quad\text{and}\quad C^+_{\max}(\sigma) \leq C^+_{\max}(\sigma_{(v)}),
\end{equation}
and consequently $\mathbf{C}_{\max}(\sigma) \leq_{R} \mathbf{C}_{\max}(\sigma_{(v)})$ for every admissible ranking~$R$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix $*\in\{-,+\}$. Since $(x,y)$ is not extreme critical, no critical path of $G^*(\sigma)$ contains it; any such path, of length $C^*_{\max}(\sigma)$, remains a valid path in $G^*(\sigma_{(v)})$ (whose only arc change is $(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)$), hence $C^*_{\max}(\sigma_{(v)})\ge C^*_{\max}(\sigma)$. Applying this to both extreme graphs gives $\mathbf{C}_{\max}(\sigma)\le_2\mathbf{C}_{\max}(\sigma_{(v)})$, and since every admissible $R$ refines $\le_2$, the claim follows.
\end{proof}

\medskip

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor1}
Neighbours in $H'(\sigma) \setminus H(\sigma)$ can never improve the makespan under any of the four rankings considered.
\end{corollary}

Corollary~\ref{cor1} justifies using $H(\sigma)$ instead of the larger $H'(\sigma)$: the reduction is gained at zero cost in solution quality.

\subsection{Feasibility}
\label{sec4.3}

\begin{theorem}[Feasibility]
\label{thm1}
For every feasible $\sigma \in \Sigma$, the reversal of any extreme critical arc $v = (x,y) \in D(\sigma)$ produces a feasible processing order $\sigma_{(v)} \in \Sigma$. Hence $H(\sigma) \subseteq \Sigma$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $G(\sigma_{(v)})$ contains a directed cycle. Since $G(\sigma)$ is acyclic and only $(x,y)$ has been removed and $(y,x)$ added, the cycle traverses $(y,x)$, yielding a directed path $\pi$ from $x$ to $y$ in $G(\sigma)$ not using $(x,y)$. In standard JSP, $x$ and $y$ lie on the same machine but in different jobs, so no precedence arc joins them; $\pi$ has at least one intermediate operation $u$, and its length in $G^*(\sigma)$ satisfies
\[
  \ell(\pi) \;=\; p_x^* + p_u^* + \cdots \;>\; p_x^*.
\]
But $(x,y)$ lies on a critical path of $G^*(\sigma)$, so the head of $y$ is tight: $r_y^*=r_x^*+p_x^*$. The path $\pi$ then gives $r_y^*\ge r_x^*+\ell(\pi)>r_x^*+p_x^*=r_y^*$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Connectivity}
\label{sec4.4}

\begin{theorem}[Connectivity]
\label{thm2}
For every non-optimal $\sigma \in \Sigma$, there exists a finite sequence of moves in $H(\sigma)$ leading to a globally optimal order $\sigma^{\star}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix any globally optimal $\sigma^*\in\Sigma^*$ and define the \emph{inversion count}
\[
  M(\sigma,\sigma^*) \;=\; \bigl|\{(x,y)\in D(\sigma) : (y,x)\in D(\sigma^*)\}\bigr|,
\]
the number of machine-order disagreements; $M=0$ iff $\sigma=\sigma^*$.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Key lemma.} If $\lambda$ is non-optimal, there exists an extreme critical $(x,y)\in D(\lambda)$ with $(y,x)\in D(\sigma^*)$.

\noindent\textit{Proof.} Non-optimality implies $\mathbf{C}_{\max}(\lambda)\not\le_2 \mathbf{C}_{\max}(\sigma^*)$ (else $\le_R$ follows by refinement), so WLOG $C^-_{\max}(\lambda)>C^-_{\max}(\sigma^*)$. Let $P$ be a critical path in $G^-(\lambda)$. If every disjunctive arc of $P$ lay in $D(\sigma^*)$, $P$ would be a valid path in $G^-(\sigma^*)$ of length $>C^-_{\max}(\sigma^*)$, a contradiction. So $P$ contains some $(x,y)\in D(\lambda)\setminus D(\sigma^*)$; since machine orientations are mutually exclusive, $(y,x)\in D(\sigma^*)$, and $(x,y)$ is extreme critical by membership in $P$. \hfill$\square_{\text{lemma}}$

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Construction.} Build $\lambda_0=\sigma$; while $\lambda_k$ is non-optimal, apply the key lemma to obtain $(x,y)$ and set $\lambda_{k+1}=(\lambda_k)_{(x,y)}\in H(\lambda_k)$ (feasible by Theorem~\ref{thm1}). Each reversal removes $(x,y)$ from $D(\lambda_k)$ (one fewer inversion) and adds $(y,x)$ (already in $D(\sigma^*)$, no new inversion), so $M(\lambda_{k+1},\sigma^*)=M(\lambda_k,\sigma^*)-1$. Since $M\ge 0$, the loop terminates in at most $M(\sigma,\sigma^*)$ steps at a globally optimal $\lambda_k$.
\end{proof}


\section{Neighbourhood Variants for IJSP}
\label{sec5}

I instantiate five concrete neighbourhood variants within the $H(\sigma)$ framework, differing in which subset of extreme critical arcs each one considers. Within a critical block $B = (o_1,\ldots,o_k)$, an arc $(o_i,o_{i+1})$ is a \emph{boundary arc} if $i=1$ or $i=k-1$, and an \emph{interior arc} otherwise. Figure~\ref{fig2} illustrates a critical block and the arcs included by each variant.

All five variants inherit the three structural guarantees of Section~\ref{sec4} (feasibility, no-loss of improving neighbours, and connectivity) for their arc-swap component, either directly (when only interior arcs are excluded, by Proposition~\ref{prop2}) or by superset relation to a variant that does. $N_8$ additionally introduces reinsertion moves that fall outside the framework; their feasibility is verified at runtime, and $N_8$ is therefore included as an exploratory variant alongside the four arc-swap variants.

\subsection{$N_1$: All Extreme Critical Arcs}
\label{sec5.1}

$N_1$ includes every disjunctive arc $(x,y)$ such that $x$ immediately precedes $y$ on the same machine in $G(\sigma)$ \emph{and} the arc lies on a critical path of $G^-(\sigma)$ or $G^+(\sigma)$. No positional filter is applied within the block: both boundary and interior arcs of every extreme critical block are candidates. Generation cost: $O(|E|)$ per extreme graph.

\subsection{$N_2$: Block-Boundary Filter}
\label{sec5.2}

$N_2$ restricts $N_1$ to arcs at the \emph{boundary} of each extreme critical block. A block $B = \{o_1,\ldots,o_k\}$ is a maximal contiguous subsequence of operations on the same machine that all lie on a single extreme critical path. $N_2$ retains the two boundary arcs $(o_1,o_2)$ and $(o_{k-1},o_k)$ and discards interior arcs $(o_i,o_{i+1})$, $2 \leq i \leq k-2$. This is the direct adaptation of \cite{Nowicki1996} to the interval setting: by Proposition~\ref{prop2} applied simultaneously to $G^-(\sigma)$ and $G^+(\sigma)$, the discarded interior arcs cannot carry improving moves under any of the four rankings, so $N_2$ inherits the same theoretical guarantees as $N_1$.

\subsection{$N_3$: Subsequence Reversals}
\label{sec5.3}

$N_3$ augments the $N_2$ boundary arc swaps with three rearrangement types applied to \emph{boundary triples} of consecutive extreme-critical tasks. For each triple $(x, y, z)$ of successive operations on the same machine that are all extreme critical and span a block boundary (i.e.\ $x$ is the first task in its block or $z$ is the last), $N_3$ generates three candidate moves: a cyclic left-shift ($x,y,z\to y,z,x$), a cyclic right-shift ($x,y,z\to z,x,y$), and a full reversal ($x,y,z\to z,y,x$). Each candidate is checked for feasibility before being retained. The resulting neighbourhood strictly contains $N_2$.

\subsection{$N_{\rm ext}$: Extended Nowicki--Smutnicki Neighbourhood}
\label{sec5.4}

$N_{\rm ext}$ adapts the extended neighbourhood of \cite{Nowicki2005}. It starts from $N_2$ (boundary arcs unconditionally) and additionally considers interior block arcs that pass a heads-and-tails viability check, denoted \texttt{isViableSwap}:
\begin{itemize}
\item Boundary arcs $(o_1,o_2)$ and $(o_{k-1},o_k)$ are always included (as in $N_2$).
\item Interior arcs $(o_i, o_{i+1})$ with $2 \leq i \leq k-2$ are included only if the $\texttt{isViableSwap}$ check returns true.
\end{itemize}
The check \texttt{isViableSwap}$(x,y)$ computes an optimistic lower-bound estimate of the post-swap makespan by recalculating only the local heads and tails of $x$ and $y$. Because the estimate is a lower bound, discarding a swap when it exceeds the incumbent is theoretically safe.

\subsection{$N_8$: Block-Boundary Swaps with Extra-Block Reinsertion}
\label{sec5.5}

$N_8$ is an exploratory variant that extends $N_2$ with \emph{extra-block reinsertion} moves, adapting the neighbourhood of~\cite{Xie2022} to the interval setting. For a critical block $B = (o_1,\ldots,o_k)$ on a machine, with machine predecessor $o_0$ (if any) and machine successor $o_{k+1}$ (if any), $N_8$ includes all $N_2$ boundary arc swaps and additionally considers four reinsertion candidates per block:
\begin{itemize}
\item move $o_1$ to just after $o_k$ (between $o_k$ and $o_{k+1}$);
\item move $o_k$ to just before $o_1$ (between $o_0$ and $o_1$);
\item move $o_1$ to the head of the machine (before all tasks on that machine);
\item move $o_k$ to the tail of the machine (after all tasks on that machine).
\end{itemize}
Each reinsertion candidate is evaluated by updating heads and tails via a localised BFS propagation; it is retained only if the resulting makespan midpoint improves on the current incumbent.

The motivation is the empirical question of whether extra-block moves benefit interval scheduling. As shown in Section~\ref{sec6.5}, the answer is search-strategy dependent: $N_8$ underperforms under hill climbing (Phase~A) but matches $N_2$ under irace-tuned tabu search (Phase~B); Section~\ref{sec7.4} explains why.


\section{Experimental Study}
\label{sec6}

The experimental study is organised in three phases that address distinct questions.
\begin{description}
\item[Phase A (Section~\ref{sec6.2}).] At a \emph{common} hyperparameter setting, I compare the four ranking operators on five neighbourhoods ($5 \times 4$ design), focusing on solution quality, the width of the returned interval, and the structural mechanism behind the LEX2--width effect.
\item[Phase B (Section~\ref{sec6.5}).] After an irace-based tuning of the tabu search local search separately for each of the five neighbourhoods, I compare the five neighbourhoods on equal footing. The tuning isolates the structural effect of the neighbourhood from confounding parameter choices.
\item[Phase C (Section~\ref{sec6.7}).] I compare the best Phase~B configuration (TS-$N_2$) against three published IJSP metaheuristics (GA, $ABC_{E3}$, ESABC), providing external validation of the proposed approach.
\end{description}

\subsection{Setup Common to All Phases}
\label{sec6.1}

\paragraph{Instances.} The benchmark suite contains 82 instances split into two groups. The first group (\emph{classical benchmarks}) comprises 12 instances selected from the OR-Library \cite{Beasley1990}: abz7, abz8, abz9 ($20\times15$); ft10 ($10\times10$), ft20 ($20\times5$); la21, la24, la25 ($15\times10$), la27, la29 ($20\times10$), la38, la40 ($15\times15$). The second group comprises 70 instances from the Taillard benchmark \cite{Taillard1993}, covering seven size classes with 10 instances each: $15\times15$ (225 operations), $20\times15$ (300), $20\times20$ (400), $30\times15$ (450), $30\times20$ (600), $50\times15$ (750) and $50\times20$ (1\,000). All 82 instances are derived from their deterministic counterparts by perturbing each nominal processing time $p_{ij}^{*}$ with a $\pm7.5\%$ uniform symmetric interval, $\mathbf{p}_{ij} = [0.925\,p_{ij}^{*},\, 1.075\,p_{ij}^{*}]$ (15\% relative width).

\paragraph{Hardware and software.} All experiments were executed on an Intel Xeon E5-2680\,v4 workstation (14 cores / 28 threads, 2.40\,GHz, 16\,GB RAM) running Windows~10 with a WSL2 Linux environment. The fEABC algorithm was implemented in C++14 and compiled with GCC using optimisation flags \texttt{-O3 -march=native -mtune=native -ffast-math}. To reduce wall-clock time, up to 24 independent runs were dispatched in parallel (one process per thread). Hyperparameter tuning (Phase~B) used the irace package~\cite{Lopez2016} (R~4.5.3), with a budget of 1\,500 candidate evaluations per neighbourhood and a Friedman-test racing criterion; the tuning was parallelised over 24 cores and required approximately five days of wall-clock time across the five neighbourhoods. The subsequent Phase~B evaluation (12\,300 runs, 15-minute time limit per run) required approximately four additional days. Statistical analysis was performed in R with paired Wilcoxon tests and Holm--Bonferroni correction. To calibrate this cluster against the hardware used in the published IJSP solvers compared in Section~\ref{sec6.7}, I ran the Phase~A fEABC+HC configuration---identical in algorithm and parameters to the one reported in~\cite{DiazFEABC2023}, which used an Intel Xeon Gold~6132 at 2.60\,GHz under native Linux---on the same 12 classical instances and compared average runtimes: the ratio is approximately $2\times$ (geometric mean 2.10, range 1.4--2.8), indicating this cluster runs the identical workload roughly twice as slow. Runtime figures in Section~\ref{sec6.7} should be interpreted accordingly; RE\,(\%) comparisons are hardware-independent.

\paragraph{Algorithm.} All configurations use \emph{fEABC}~\cite{DiazFEABC2023}, a population-based hybrid metaheuristic that combines an Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) framework, Particle Swarm Optimisation (PSO) velocity updates for solution generation, and an embedded local search applied at each generation to a fraction of the population, with Lamarckian propagation of improvements. Solutions are encoded as permutations with repetition (\emph{job-order}) and decoded via an insertion-based Schedule Generation Scheme (SGS); the population contains 250 individuals. The local search component differs by phase: Phase~A uses first-improvement hill climbing as a neutral, operator-agnostic baseline; Phase~B uses a tabu search (best-neighbour selection with a short-term tabu list) independently tuned by irace for each neighbourhood (Section~\ref{sec6.5}; the rationale for this switch is discussed there). The stopping criterion differs by phase: Phase~A terminates after 25 consecutive generations without improvement in the global best; Phase~B terminates after 20 such generations or after a wall-clock limit of 900\,s (15~minutes) per run, whichever is reached first. Thirty independent runs are executed for every (instance, configuration) pair.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter configuration.}
Phase~A adopts the configuration validated in the fEABC paper~\cite{DiazFEABC2023}: JOX (Job Order Crossover), inversion mutation, food-source abandonment limit $\text{NTmax}=20$ (maximum trials before a source is abandoned), elite size~40, and first-improvement hill-climbing local search applied to 75\% of individuals per generation.

Phase~B replaces hill climbing with tabu search and tunes six parameters independently per neighbourhood using irace~\cite{Lopez2016}: crossover operator, mutation operator, elite size, NTmax, local-search target fraction, and the bad-iteration limit (maximum consecutive non-improving tabu iterations). The remaining parameters are fixed: population~250, tabu-list minimum size~3, and LEX2 ranking. LEX2 is chosen because Phase~A shows it produces makespan intervals 5--11\% narrower than the alternatives (Table~\ref{tab3}) with no significant difference in midpoint quality versus EV ($p = 0.197$, $r = 0.026$, Table~\ref{tab2}); fixing it allows the neighbourhood comparison in Phase~B to be conducted under the operator that minimises solution uncertainty at no cost in makespan quality. Table~\ref{tab:tuned} reports the resulting configurations.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Comparisons use the Friedman test (non-parametric repeated-measures ANOVA; the statistic follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with $k-1$ degrees of freedom for $k$ groups) followed by pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm--Bonferroni correction. Effect size is reported as $r = |Z| / \sqrt{N}$, where $Z$ is the standardised Wilcoxon test statistic and $N$ is the number of paired observations, with the conventional thresholds (small $\geq 0.1$, medium $\geq 0.3$, large $\geq 0.5$). Each (instance, run) pair forms a paired block. The metric is the midpoint of the makespan interval, $(C^-_{\max} + C^+_{\max})/2$, which is a neutral common ground for all four operators.

\subsection{Phase A: Operator Calibration}
\label{sec6.2}

Phase A is preparatory rather than central: its purpose is to identify the ranking operator that minimises makespan interval uncertainty at negligible midpoint cost, so that it can be fixed for the neighbourhood comparison in Phase~B. The $5 \times 4$ design (five neighbourhoods, four operators, common first-improvement hill-climbing local search) gives $49{,}200$ runs and also establishes a neutral baseline that Phase~B will contrast against.

\paragraph{Midpoint quality.} The Friedman test across operators yields $\chi^{2}(3) = 694.96$, $p < 10^{-150}$. Table~\ref{tab1} reports the global mean midpoint and median runtime; pairwise tests (Table~\ref{tab2}) show that LEX1 is significantly worse than the other three (medium-to-large effects), EV and LEX2 are statistically indistinguishable ($p = 0.197$, $r = 0.026$), and YX leads on average but its advantage over EV and LEX2 carries only small effects ($r \leq 0.131$). The choice of operator has no meaningful effect on wall-clock time: Kruskal--Wallis tests return $p > 0.48$ for four of the five neighbourhoods, and the only significant case ($N_{\rm ext}$, $p = 0.005$) involves absolute differences of $\leq 52$\,s. The dominant driver of runtime is neighbourhood structure, not operator ($N_3$ is 5--8$\times$ slower than $N_2$ at the median regardless of operator).

\paragraph{Interval width and its mechanism.} The width $C^+_{\max} - C^-_{\max}$ measures solution uncertainty~\cite{Moore2009,Lei2012c}. Table~\ref{tab3} shows that LEX2 yields intervals 5.3\% narrower than EV and 10.7\% narrower than LEX1; the ordering LEX2 $\prec$ YX $\prec$ EV $\prec$ LEX1 holds across every neighbourhood. The effect is consistent at the instance level: LEX2 produces narrower intervals than LEX1 in 88.8\%, than EV in 72.2\%, and than YX in 70.5\% of (instance, neighbourhood) pairs. Figure~\ref{fig3}(a) confirms the distributional shift; panels~(b) and~(c) reveal its mechanism. The natural hypothesis is that LEX2 leaves more structural slack in $G^+(\sigma)$, letting the worst-case path shorten. The float analysis refutes this: LEX2 solutions have \emph{more} critical operations and \emph{less} per-operation float in $G^+(\sigma)$, the opposite of the slack hypothesis. The mechanism is directional: LEX2 accepts moves that reduce $C^+_{\max}$ even when $C^-_{\max}$ stays unchanged, directly tightening the upper-bound graph. This asymmetry between the two extreme graphs is LEX2's structural fingerprint; Section~\ref{sec7.2} provides the theoretical account.

\subsection{Phase B: Neighbourhood Comparison after Tuning}
\label{sec6.5}

Phase B is the central experimental contribution. Each neighbourhood is given its own irace-tuned tabu search configuration (Table~\ref{tab:tuned}), isolating structural neighbourhood differences from confounding parameter choices. The switch from hill climbing to tabu search is necessary: hill climbing converges quickly to a local optimum and then stalls, suppressing any structural difference between neighbourhoods; tabu search escapes local optima throughout the full time budget, allowing each neighbourhood's block geometry and candidate-set size to influence the search trajectory. Using the irace-tuned configurations, I evaluate the five neighbourhoods on the 82 benchmark instances (30 runs per instance, $5 \times 82 \times 30 = 12{,}300$ observations); the most notable configuration difference is that irace selected GOX crossover for $N_3$---the only variant using subsequence reversals---while all others retained JOX, and $N_3$ received the highest bad-iteration budget~(30), consistent with its larger candidate set.

Figure~\ref{fig5}(a) shows the per-instance relative error RE\,(\%):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RE}\,(\%) = \frac{E[\mathbf{C}_{\max}] - \mathrm{LB}}{\mathrm{LB}} \times 100,
\label{eq:re}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{LB}$ is the published lower bound from the literature \cite{Taillard1993,Beasley1990,DiazFEABC2023}. This single metric is used uniformly throughout Sections~\ref{sec6.5}--\ref{sec6.7}. The Friedman test yields $\chi^{2}(4) = 4120.58,\, p < 10^{-150}$; mean ranks are $N_2$: 2.143, $N_8$: 2.245, $N_{\rm ext}$: 2.638, $N_1$: 3.419, $N_3$: 4.555. \subsection{Comparison with Published IJSP Solvers}
\label{sec6.7}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec7}

\subsection{Practical Recommendations}
\label{sec7.1}

The experimental results lead to a small set of actionable recommendations:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Choose between $N_2$ and $N_8$ based on instance size and time budget. $N_2$ is the default recommendation: it ties with $N_8$ for the best quality overall (negligible difference, $r = 0.07$) and is faster for small and medium instances (Table~\ref{tab:phaseb_groups}). On the tai$50{\times}15$ class, $N_8$ achieves the strictly lower group mean RE\,(\%) (0.22\% vs $N_2$'s 0.32\%) at a moderate extra runtime (218\,s vs 181\,s), consistent with its structural advantage in the single-dominant-critical-path regime \cite{Xie2022}; for tai$50{\times}20$, $N_2$ recovers the edge (2.03\% vs $N_8$'s 2.11\%, 554\,s vs 603\,s). $N_8$ is thus the preferred choice when quality is paramount on the largest 50-job instances with 15 machines, while $N_2$ is more robust across the full range.
\item Do not assume $N_{\rm ext}$ is best on the basis of Phase~A results. Under irace-tuned tabu search, $N_{\rm ext}$ finishes third---a medium effect behind both $N_2$ and $N_8$---and becomes the \emph{slowest} viable neighbourhood on large instances (tai$50{\times}20$: 695\,s vs $N_2$'s 554\,s and $N_8$'s 603\,s, at a 0.79\,pp quality cost; Table~\ref{tab:phaseb_groups}). The heads-and-tails viability filter that makes $N_{\rm ext}$ efficient under hill climbing becomes a runtime bottleneck under tabu search with a full neighbourhood evaluation per iteration.
\item Use LEX2 if interval width is the goal; operator choice has limited impact on midpoint. LEX2 yields makespan intervals 5--11\% narrower than the alternatives on average, with structural evidence for this effect (Section~\ref{sec7.2}), and this advantage is consistent across prior work that includes per-operator hyperparameter tuning \cite{DiazICAE2023,DiazIGPL2022}.
\item Avoid $N_3$ in either setting. The 3-task block rearrangements it adds beyond $N_2$ are dominated on both dimensions: it is the weakest neighbourhood on solution quality and the most expensive computationally, reaching the 900\,s time limit for the largest instances.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Theoretical Connection: Why LEX2 Narrows Intervals}
\label{sec7.2}

Proposition~\ref{prop2} guarantees that reversing a non-extreme-critical arc cannot improve either bound. LEX2 specifically targets $C^+_{\max}$, accepting moves that reduce $C^+_{\max}$ even when $C^-_{\max}$ stays put. Over many local search steps, this creates a bias toward configurations where $G^+(\sigma)$ has a shorter critical path --- and, by the structure of interval arithmetic, a tighter bound on the overall interval width. The float analysis of Section~\ref{sec6.2} provides direct empirical confirmation: LEX2 solutions have more critical operations in $G^+(\sigma)$ and less per-operation slack, while $G^-(\sigma)$ becomes relatively looser. The asymmetry between the two extreme graphs is a structural fingerprint of the LEX2 ranking rule.

\subsection{Why $N_3$ Underperforms}
\label{sec7.3}

$N_3$ is counter-intuitive: it is a strict superset of $N_2$ in the move-generation sense (boundary arc swaps plus three types of 3-task block rearrangements---cyclic left-shift, cyclic right-shift, and full reversal of boundary triples), yet it produces worse solutions. Each 3-task rearrangement candidate requires a full feasibility evaluation to detect cycles before it can be retained, which significantly increases the per-iteration cost. More importantly, these extra candidates do not carry improving moves in practice: the boundary arc swaps already capture the structurally meaningful transitions (as justified by Proposition~\ref{prop2}), and the 3-task rearrangements add volume to the candidate pool without adding quality. Under best-improvement selection, a larger pool diluted with poor-quality candidates reduces the effective yield per evaluation, resulting in slower convergence to a worse plateau.

\subsection{Why $N_8$ Performs Well Under Tabu Search}
\label{sec7.4}

$N_8$ extends $N_2$ with extra-block reinsertion moves filtered by a midpoint lower-bound estimate. Under hill climbing (Phase~A), this pairing is counter-productive in the interval setting: the midpoint filter discards moves that reduce $C^+_{\max}$ without improving $C^-_{\max}$---precisely those that exploit the disagreement between $G^-(\sigma)$ and $G^+(\sigma)$---so hill climbing spends budget evaluating reinsertion candidates that rarely improve the interval criterion. The result is slower convergence at lower yield compared with $N_2$, as visible in Figure~\ref{fig6}.

Under tabu search (Phase~B), this penalty reverses. Three mechanisms interact. First, the tabu list prevents revisiting recently-moved operations, forcing the search to explore the full breadth of $N_8$'s candidate set---including the reinsertion moves---rather than cycling near a local optimum where only arc swaps are informative. Second, tabu search employs best-improvement selection: among all candidate moves at each iteration (arc swaps and reinsertions alike), the globally best is chosen; in a larger neighbourhood this captures improvements that first-improvement hill climbing would miss. Third, the aspiration criterion allows a tabu move to be accepted if it improves the current best solution; this creates a channel through which reinsertion moves that improve the interval criterion in aggregate (even at a mid-iteration midpoint cost) are eventually accepted.

In the deterministic setting, the mechanism is simpler: with a single critical path, a reinsertion that improves the midpoint also improves the makespan, so the filter is calibrated and extra-block moves help under both hill climbing and tabu search~\cite{Xie2022}. The practical implication is that $N_8$ is a neighbourhood whose benefit is conditional on the search strategy: it requires a memory-based mechanism to exploit its enlarged candidate set effectively.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec7.5}

\begin{itemize}
\item Larger instances and per-operator tuning. Extending to tai$100{\times}20$ (2000 operations) would require excessively long runtimes on the current cluster, but would be valuable: larger search spaces tend to sharpen structural differences between neighbourhoods, and the question of whether operator differences persist under per-operator tuning remains open beyond the 12 small instances studied in \cite{DiazICAE2023,DiazIGPL2022}.
\item Other uncertainty levels. The 15\% uniform interval width is a single noise level; sensitivity to other noise widths is left open.
\item Hybrid algorithms. $({\rm LEX2}, N_2)$ is the natural starting point for a dedicated uncertainty-aware metaheuristic: LEX2 directly tightens the worst-case bound (Section~\ref{sec7.2}) and $N_2$ provides the strongest neighbourhood structure overall; $({\rm LEX2}, N_8)$ is a natural alternative for large instances. A dedicated algorithm that exploits the structural mechanism of Section~\ref{sec7.2}---directly optimising moves that tighten $G^+(\sigma)$---could improve on the general-purpose tabu search used here.
\item Other rankings. Additional admissible orders on intervals \cite{Bustince2013} could be tested with the same protocol; the structural mechanism uncovered for LEX2 should generalise to any operator that strictly prioritises one extreme.
\end{itemize}


\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec8}

I have presented a theoretical framework for neighbourhood-based local search in the Interval Job Shop Scheduling Problem grounded in the concept of \emph{extreme critical paths} --- arcs that are critical in either the best-case or worst-case scenario graph. The resulting neighbourhood $H(\sigma)$ satisfies feasibility, connectivity and the no-loss-of-improving-neighbours property for all four admissible interval rankings considered (EV, LEX1, LEX2, YX) simultaneously.

Five neighbourhood variants ($N_1$, $N_2$, $N_3$, $N_{\rm ext}$, $N_8$) and four ranking operators were evaluated on 82 benchmark instances of up to 1000 operations, across three phases: a $5 \times 4$ comparison at a common parameter setting, a five-way comparison after irace-based tuning, and a comparison with three published IJSP metaheuristics.

After per-neighbourhood irace-tuned tabu search, $N_2$ and $N_8$ emerge as the two strongest variants---statistically tied (negligible effect $r=0.07$)---while $N_{\rm ext}$, $N_1$, and $N_3$ trail by medium, large, and very large effects respectively. Considered jointly with runtime, $N_2$ is the most efficient choice across all instance sizes: it ties for best quality, is faster than $N_{\rm ext}$ for all instances with $\geq 450$ operations, and is faster than $N_8$ for small and medium instances. $N_8$ is the preferred choice for large instances when runtimes of 200--600\,s are acceptable, as it achieves the best quality on the tai$50{\times}15$ class. Regarding ranking operators, LEX2 yields makespan intervals 5--11\% narrower than the alternatives on average. A float analysis of both extreme graphs provides structural support for this finding: LEX2 solutions show more critical operations and less per-operation slack in $G^+(\sigma)$, consistent with LEX2 directly tightening the worst-case critical path rather than leaving more structural slack. The $N_8$ variant's rise to the top tier under tabu search---in contrast to its mid-table performance under hill climbing---is a key finding: extra-block reinsertion moves provide no advantage under first-improvement hill climbing (the cost exceeds the gain), but become genuinely useful under tabu search, where the memory mechanism exploits the enlarged candidate set without cycling. This underscores that the choice of local-search strategy interacts strongly with neighbourhood design in the interval setting. The comparison against three published IJSP metaheuristics (GA, $ABC_{E3}$, ESABC) provides external validation of the approach: the irace-tuned TS-$N_2$ achieves strictly lower average RE\,(\%) than the previous best solver on every one of the 82 benchmark instances, with mean reductions of 56.6\% on the 12 classical instances and 57.7\% on the 70 Taillard instances.

These results provide actionable guidance for practitioners implementing local search for uncertain job shop scheduling: the choice of ranking operator can be used not only to optimise expected makespan but also to directly control solution uncertainty, and the choice of neighbourhood should account for whether the problem is genuinely interval-valued or only nominally uncertain.
